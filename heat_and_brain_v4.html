Heat and the Brain: Heat Exposure vs. Neurological Care Access in Rural Counties Near Dallas

HOSA project draft. Updated 2026-11-18. All six measures have data for all five counties. Results are preliminary: Table C sources must still be opened and checked.

Executive Summary

Question. Which rural counties near Dallas combine extreme heat with limited access to neurological and emergency care, and which groups are most exposed?

Approach. Five Texas counties (Dallas as the urban comparison; Navarro, Henderson, Hunt and Wise as rural counties) were compared on six public-data measures: extreme-heat days, share of residents age 65+, share of households with no vehicle, stroke rate, neurologists per 100,000 people, and driving distance to the nearest emergency room. Each measure was ranked, grouped into Heat, Vulnerability and Access gap, and averaged with equal weights.

Key findings (preliminary).

  • Navarro County ranks highest (3.75 of 5). It has no neurologists on record, the highest stroke rate (3.7%), and moderate-to-high heat (16.0 days at or above 100°F per year).
  • Navarro stayed in first place under every alternative weighting tested (see Robustness Check). With heat counted double, Dallas came within 0.02 points.
  • Wise County has the longest drive to an ER (6.6 miles, about 16 minutes) and the second-most heat days among the rural counties (18.3 per year).
  • Dallas is the hottest county (23.7 days per year) but has the shortest drive to an ER and 10.0 neurologists per 100,000.
  • Henderson has the oldest population (22.4% age 65+) but the fewest heat days (10.6, probably an undercount).
  • Two of the four rural counties (Navarro and Hunt) have no neurologists on record. The other two have two each.

Caution. This shows association, not causation. Rural neurologist counts are tiny, one weather station represents each county, and the score weights are a choice. See Limitations.

In rural counties near Dallas, which areas combine high heat exposure with low access to neurological and emergency care, and which patient groups are most at risk?

2. Hypothesis

Rural counties with more extreme-heat days, fewer neurologists and farther emergency rooms will have higher risk for people with neurological conditions.

9. Method: Heat-Risk and Access-Gap Score

  1. Rank each county on each measure (1 = lowest risk, 5 = highest risk). More heat days, older population, more households without a vehicle, higher stroke rate, fewer neurologists per 100,000, and longer ER distance all rank as higher risk. Ties share the average rank.
  2. Average the ranks within three groups: Heat (days over 100°F), Vulnerability (age 65+, no vehicle, stroke rate), and Access gap (neurologists per 100,000, ER distance).
  3. Average the three group scores with equal weights to get the total. Equal weights are a simple starting choice and are tested below.
  4. Map the score in QGIS or Google My Maps and choose the 1–2 highest-risk counties for case studies.

11. Discussion and Recommendations

What the data suggests. The county with the most heat (Dallas) is not the county with the highest combined risk, because Dallas has strong neurological and emergency care. The highest combined risk appears where moderate heat meets the lowest neurologist access (Navarro). Wise stands out for the longest drive to an ER, which matters most in emergencies such as heat stroke or a seizure.

Ideas to evaluate (these are hypotheses, not findings).

  • Telehealth neurology options for counties with no neurologists on record (Navarro, Hunt).
  • Heat-safety and medication guidance aimed at older adults, especially in Henderson, where 22.4% of residents are 65+.
  • EMS coverage and response time review for Wise County, given the longest ER drive.
  • Cooling-center and transport outreach for households without a vehicle, especially in Dallas County (6.8%).

Each idea needs a source or a local check (for example, whether a county already runs cooling centers) before it goes into a final recommendation.

12. Limitations

  • Shows association, not causation.
  • One weather station stands in for each county. Henderson's station (Athens) has missing days, so its heat count may be too low. Dallas's station is in the city, which adds urban heat.
  • Neurologist counts are very small (0–2) in rural counties, so one doctor moving changes the rank. Residents may also see neurologists in neighboring counties.
  • Stroke is one condition and stands in for neurological burden. It differs little between counties (2.9–3.7%).
  • ER distance is measured from each county's geographic center, not from where people live. It uses OpenStreetMap routing, which may differ from Google Maps.
  • The CMS hospital list left out Wise County's hospital, so Medical City Decatur was added by hand. Three candidate hospitals could not be located, and some locations came from a geocoder that was not checked by hand.
  • Rankings across only five counties cannot show how far apart the counties really are.
  • Equal weights in the score are a choice, not a finding.
