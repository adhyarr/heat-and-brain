1. Existing Government Initiatives

Both state and federal agencies invest substantial funding in summer cooling assistance, emergency preparedness, and rural stroke and emergency care.

Texas State Programs
  • CEAP / LIHEAP Cooling Assistance (TDHCA): Texas allocates roughly half of its federal LIHEAP energy funds through the Comprehensive Energy Assistance Program (CEAP) to summer electricity bills, shutoff crisis relief, fans or AC units, and HVAC repair through about 30 local agencies.
  • 2-1-1 Texas and Local Cooling Centers (HHSC / TDEM): Dialing 2-1-1 connects residents to local cooling centers (libraries, senior centers, community halls), emergency shelters, and utility help.
  • Texas DSHS Stroke and Trauma System: Certifies hospitals across four stroke levels (Level I Comprehensive through Level IV Acute Stroke Ready) so local ERs and EMS crews have clear emergency transfer protocols.
U.S. Federal Programs
  • NIHHIS / Heat.gov and CDC-NWS HeatRisk: A multi-agency portal (NOAA, CDC, HHS, and FEMA) offering the 24-hour color-coded HeatRisk forecast and the county-level CDC Heat and Health Tracker.
  • HHS LIHEAP Weatherization and Cooling: Federal block grants helping low-income older adults and residents with disabilities afford home air conditioning and summer utility costs.
  • HRSA Telehealth and Telestroke Grants: Funds 24/7 video links connecting rural emergency departments with specialists at major medical centers for rapid imaging review and emergency treatment support.
2. The Remaining Last-Mile Gap Even with significant federal and state investment in HeatRisk, CEAP, and emergency telehealth, this 24-county, 1,885-tract study shows three practical gaps that still affect rural residents:
  • Heat alerts do not reflect local care access: National HeatRisk forecasts do not account for the fact that 12 of 20 rural counties have zero neurologists on record, or that 100% of residents in 8 rural counties live more than 60 minutes from a Texas DSHS Level I or II stroke center.
  • Hospital telehealth starts only after arrival: Telestroke cannot shorten long out-of-county emergency drives (such as Van Zandt County's 42.3-minute average drive to the nearest ER) or solve transit barriers for households without a vehicle (6.2% in Hill County).
  • Daily medication and cooling gaps remain: Many older adults taking diuretics, blood-pressure drugs, or anticholinergics never receive a pre-summer medication checkup before extreme heat arrives.

3. Recommended Interventions

Practical steps combining individual and caregiver preparation with primary-care, EMS, and local policy action.

Individual, Caregiver, and Community Steps
  • Pre-summer medication checkup: Ask a primary-care doctor or pharmacist before May to flag prescriptions that affect sweating, thirst, or blood pressure (diuretics, ACE inhibitors, ARBs, anticholinergics, antipsychotics, stimulants, and tricyclic antidepressants).
  • Heat-safe medication storage: Keep epilepsy, multiple sclerosis, Parkinson's, and blood-pressure medications out of hot vehicles and direct sunlight.
  • Twice-daily wellness checks: Check on adults aged 65+ and people with dementia during 100°F+ heat waves, especially in counties with large senior shares (Hood 25.4%, Rains 24.2%, Henderson 22.4%).
  • Early cooling and 911 planning: Save 2-1-1 Texas and county transit numbers before summer, and call 911 right away for confusion, slurred speech, or a body temperature above 104°F.
Local, State, and Health-System Policy
  • Pair HeatRisk with care-access scores: Target emergency outreach and cooling resources to high-heat, high-access-gap counties such as Hill, Navarro, Erath, and Hood.
  • Expand outpatient telehealth across specialties: Extend HRSA telehealth support to routine primary-care and neurology video visits, reducing 60 to 112 minute summer drives for rural seniors.
  • Fund 2-1-1 cooling rides and mobile sites: Provide on-demand transit vouchers for car-free seniors and set up temporary cooling stations in rural towns without municipal centers.
  • Prioritize CEAP AC aid and rural EMS cooling: Fast-track HVAC repair for medically vulnerable residents and equip rural EMS ambulances with active cooling supplies.

4. Additional Rural Infrastructure Ideas

Ways to use existing rural touchpoints (pharmacies, volunteer fire halls, utilities, and primary care) without waiting for new hospitals.

Community and Clinical Touchpoints
  • Pharmacy heat-safety labeling: Rural towns without specialists often still have a local pharmacy. Adding summer heat-warning labels and brief counseling when dispensing high-risk medications reaches patients directly.
  • Micro-cooling hubs (volunteer fire halls and churches): Pre-registering air-conditioned volunteer fire stations and churches with 2-1-1 Texas creates cooling access outside city limits.
  • Spring primary-care checklists: Handing out a 1-page heat plan during spring visits helps patients prepare before 100°F weather hits.
EMS, Utility, and Registry Coordination
  • Pre-hospital EMS stroke bypass protocols: In counties 60 to 112 minutes from Level I/II stroke centers (Hill, Navarro, Erath, Freestone), pre-hospital screening helps EMS route severe strokes directly to thrombectomy-capable hospitals or coordinate air transport.
  • Heat-triggered utility shutoff pauses: Automatically pausing residential electric disconnections whenever NWS issues a Heat Advisory protects vulnerable seniors before a crisis occurs.
  • Voluntary EMS and Meals on Wheels registries: Pairing voluntary medical lists with local 911/EMS and meal-delivery drivers enables proactive check-ins during multi-day heat waves.
