1. What Government Is Doing Today

Both Texas and federal agencies invest significant funding and run established programs for summer cooling and rural emergency stroke care.

Texas State Initiatives
  • CEAP / LIHEAP Cooling Aid (TDHCA): Unlike northern states focused on winter heating, Texas allocates roughly half of federal LIHEAP energy funds through CEAP to summer electric bills, crisis disconnection prevention, fans/AC units, and HVAC repair via ~30 local agencies.
  • 2-1-1 Texas & Local Cooling Centers (HHSC / TDEM): Dialing 2-1-1 connects residents to city/county cooling centers (libraries, senior centers, community halls), emergency shelters, and utility help.
  • TX DSHS Stroke Designation: Certifies hospitals across 4 stroke levels (Level I Comprehensive to Level IV Acute Stroke Ready) so EMS and rural ERs have formal transfer protocols.
U.S. Federal Initiatives
  • NIHHIS / Heat.gov & CDC–NWS HeatRisk: Multi-agency portal (NOAA, CDC, HHS, FEMA) providing the 24-hour color-coded HeatRisk index and county-level CDC Heat & Health Tracker.
  • HHS LIHEAP Weatherization & Cooling: Federal block grants helping low-income older adults and people with disabilities afford home air conditioning and energy bills.
  • HRSA Telestroke Grants (TNGP): Funds 24/7 video neurology links so small rural ERs can connect immediately with vascular neurologists at major stroke hubs for CT review and tPA decisions.
2. Though Government Programs & Funding Exist, a Last-Mile Gap Remains Even with major federal and state dollars behind HeatRisk, CEAP, and Telestroke, our 24-county / 1,885-tract analysis highlights a clear gap:
  • Heat alerts don't see care deserts: National HeatRisk forecasts don't factor in that 12 of 20 rural counties have 0 neurologists on record or that 100% of residents in 8 rural counties live >60 minutes from a DSHS Level I–II stroke center.
  • Telestroke only helps after ER arrival: It doesn't solve long out-of-county ER drives (such as Van Zandt's 42.3-minute average) or barriers for no-vehicle households (6.2% in Hill County).
  • Daily medication & home cooling gaps: Many older adults on diuretics, blood-pressure drugs, or anticholinergics don't receive summer heat-medication counseling before heat waves hit.

3. How We Can Improve It

Practical steps that pair personal and caregiver preparation with targeted local, state, and health-system action.

What Individuals, Caregivers & Communities Can Do
  • Annual pre-summer medication review: Ask a doctor or pharmacist before May to flag drugs affecting sweating, thirst, or blood pressure (diuretics, ACE inhibitors, ARBs, anticholinergics, antipsychotics, stimulants, TCAs).
  • Heat-safe medication storage: Keep epilepsy, MS, Parkinson's, and blood-pressure prescriptions out of hot cars and mailboxes.
  • Twice-daily buddy checks: Check on adults 65+ or living with dementia during ≥100°F stretches (especially in Hood 25.4%, Rains 24.2%, Henderson 22.4%).
  • Pre-plan cooling & rides: Save 2-1-1 Texas and county transit numbers ahead of heat waves; call 911 fast for confusion, slurred speech, or temp >104°F.
What Local, State & Federal Policy Can Do
  • Combine HeatRisk with care-access scores: Target emergency outreach to high-heat, high-access-gap counties like Hill, Navarro, Erath, and Hood.
  • Expand routine tele-neurology: Use HRSA telehealth grants for scheduled video neurology visits (not just acute stroke), saving rural seniors 60–90+ minute summer drives.
  • Free 2-1-1 cooling rides & mobile stations: Fund on-demand transit vouchers for car-free seniors and pop-up cooling sites in rural towns without municipal centers.
  • Prioritize CEAP AC aid & rural EMS cooling: Fast-track HVAC/AC assistance for residents with neurological conditions and equip rural EMS ambulances with active cooling supplies.
